Ichinomiya near Tokyo: The First Shrines of the Old Provinces

An Ichinomiya is the foremost shrine of an old province (see Shrine Names and Ranks and the basics article). Before today's 47 prefectures, Japan was divided into about 70 “provinces.”

Tokyo lies within one of those provinces, Musashi. Around it, packed tightly together, are Sagami, Kazusa, Shimosa, Awa, Hitachi, Kozuke, Shimotsuke and Kai. Tokyo is one of the most convenient starting points in Japan for visiting Ichinomiya.

This article introduces the Ichinomiya around Tokyo, one shrine per province. It also looks at the provinces where the Ichinomiya cannot be settled on a single shrine, and at how Ichinomiya have “multiplied.”

Provinces where the Ichinomiya cannot be settled on one shrine

There is no list of Ichinomiya made by the state (see Shrine Names and Ranks). So there are provinces where two shrines claim the title. Around Tokyo there are three.

Musashi: Hikawa Jinja and Ono Jinja

In general, Hikawa Jinja in Saitama is known as the Ichinomiya of Musashi.

However, Okunitama Jinja in Fuchu, the Soja of Musashi, enshrines the Ichinomiya through Rokunomiya of the province together, and the “Ichinomiya” there is Ono Jinja in Tama, while Hikawa Jinja is the “Sannomiya.”

Two ways of ranking survive within the same province. Ono Jinja is one of the shrines that claim to be the successor of a shrine listed in the Engishiki, and it stands in a place called Ichinomiya in Tama.

Shimotsuke: the Futarasan shrines of Utsunomiya and Nikko

Tochigi has two shrines called Futarasan (or Futarayama) Jinja, and both are regarded as the Ichinomiya of Shimotsuke.

  • Utsunomiya Futarayama Jinja stands in the centre of Utsunomiya.
  • Nikko Futarasan Jinja is a shrine of the mountain worship of Nikko, with Mount Nantai as its sacred object. It is part of the World Heritage Site “Shrines and Temples of Nikko.”

Awa: Awa Jinja and Susaki Jinja

In Tateyama there are two shrines regarded as the Ichinomiya of Awa: Awa Jinja and Susaki Jinja. Susaki Jinja is not on this map.

Ichinomiya have sometimes “multiplied”

Tsurugaoka Hachimangu in Kamakura is not an Ichinomiya of Sagami from old times; the traditional Ichinomiya of Sagami is Samukawa Jinja. Even so, today it is sometimes counted as the Ichinomiya of Sagami Province.

An Ichinomiya is not something fixed a thousand years ago and left unchanged. How they are counted is still shifting today.

How it looks on the map: the “Ichinomiya” colour includes the long-standing Ichinomiya, shrines such as Ono Jinja that claim to be the successor of an ancient shrine, and Tsurugaoka Hachimangu, which is sometimes counted as one today.

Kashima and Katori: the two shrines that reward the deepest reading

Of the Ichinomiya around Tokyo, the ones for which books and public sources are thickest are Kashima Jingu in Ibaraki and Katori Jingu in Chiba. They face each other across the Tone River.

  • A pair in myth. The kami of Kashima (Takemikazuchi) and of Katori (Futsunushi) are said to have been sent together to Izumo in the myth of the transfer of the land (see the myths in the basics article).
  • Both have a “kaname-ishi,” a keystone. It is said to be a stone that pins down the giant catfish beneath the ground, the cause of earthquakes.
  • Two of the few shrines called “Jingu” in the ancient register (see Shrine Names and Ranks).
  • A festival once every twelve years. Kashima's Mifune Matsuri, a boat festival, is held in the Year of the Horse. The most recent was in September 2026.
  • The Three Shrines of the East. Kashima and Katori, plus Ikisu Jinja in Ibaraki, are visited together as the “Togoku Sanja pilgrimage,” an old way of visiting that has drawn more people again in recent years. Ikisu Jinja is not on this map.

A rough way to plan: by direction

No travel times here, only directions. For how to get to each shrine, see its own page.

  • East (Ibaraki and northern Chiba): Kashima and Katori
  • South-east (Boso Peninsula): Tamasaki and Awa
  • South-west (Kanagawa): Samukawa, which can be combined with Tsurugaoka Hachimangu in Kamakura
  • North (Saitama and Tochigi): Hikawa, Utsunomiya Futarayama and Nikko Futarasan
  • North-west (Gunma): Nukisaki
  • West (Tama, Yamanashi, Fuji and Izu): Ono, Asama (Kai Ichinomiya), Fujisan Hongu Sengen and Mishima
  • Further west (Nagano): Suwa
