Shrine Names and Ranks: Jinja, Jingu, Taisha and a Thousand Years of Labels

Next to the torii, a stone pillar or signboard often carries several titles besides the shrine's name: “Musashi Ichinomiya,” “former Kanpei Taisha,” “Chokusaisha.” For Hikawa Jinja in Saitama, all of them apply.

None of them was decided at once. A register compiled by the imperial court in 927. A ranking drawn up province by province near the end of the Heian period. An imperial festival in 1868. A system of state ranks that ran from 1871 to 1946. Labels from different eras lie on one shrine like layers of sediment.

The colours on this map (Jingu, Taisha, Ichinomiya, Ninomiya) pick up some of those layers. This article peels them back one at a time, including the ones the map does not show.

Reading the end of the name: the shrine suffix (shago)

A shrine's name has two parts: the name itself, such as Hikawa or Kashima, and the shrine suffix (shago) at the end, such as Jinja, Jingu or Taisha. The suffix is the last word of the name, and it shows what kind of shrine this is.

SuffixWhat it shows
JinjaWhat it showsThe most common suffix
JinguWhat it showsA shrine linked to a deity of the Imperial Family, or enshrining an emperor (see the basics article)
TaishaWhat it showsBefore the war, only Izumo Taisha. After the war, more shrines began to use it (see the basics article)

Sha / YashiroWhat it showsSmall shrines, and shrines inside the grounds of another

In the ancient register, very few shrines were called “Jingu.” Of the roughly 2,860 shrines in the court's register of 927 (Layer 1, below), most are simply “sha.” Among the few Jingu are Kashima and Katori in the Kanto region, and both can be visited from Tokyo in a day.

Names that disappeared: Myojin and Gongen

Until the Edo period, kami were often given titles such as “Daimyojin,” “Gongen” and “Daibosatsu.” Gongen and Bosatsu are Buddhist words, and they came from the idea that a kami is a Buddha in another form (Shinto–Buddhist syncretism; see Japan and Shrines).

When the government separated kami from Buddhas at the start of the Meiji era (the separation of Shinto and Buddhism, 1868), Gongen and Bosatsu, and Myojin as well, stopped being used as titles for kami.

  • Hakone Jinja was known in the Middle Ages as “Hakone Gongen,” a sacred site where Shinto and Buddhism were joined. After the separation, it took its present name.
  • The formal name of Kanda Myojin is “Kanda Jinja.” Even so, people still call it by the name “Myojin.”

Where the name on the signboard differs from the name people use, the shrine was usually renamed at this time.

Layer 1: the court's register (927)

Shikinaisha

The Heian court compiled a register of the shrines that received offerings (heihaku) at state rituals. It forms volumes 9 and 10 of the Engishiki, a collection of regulations, and is called the Jinmyocho, the Engishiki register of shrines. It was completed in 927 and came into use in 967.

It lists 2,861 shrines and 3,132 kami, counted by deity. These are the Shikinaisha, the shrines listed in the Engishiki.

  • Not being listed does not mean a shrine is new. Many shrines that existed at the time were left out (Shikigesha).
  • Over a thousand years, shrines have moved or changed names, and for some Shikinaisha it is unclear which shrine today is the true successor. When several shrines claim to be the successor, each is called a ronsha, a claimant shrine.

Four divisions, and a special class

Shikinaisha were divided in two by who made the offering. At Kanpeisha, the Jingikan, the court's office for shrines, made it; at Kokuheisha, the provincial officials (kokushi) did. Each group had Taisha (major shrines) and Shosha (minor shrines).

From among the Taisha, some were singled out for special prayers when something went wrong in the country. These are the Myojin Taisha.

  • Example: Hikawa Jinja is listed as a Myojin Taisha.

How the register of 927 is organised

Shikinaisha → Kanpeisha or Kokuheisha → Taisha or Shosha each → some of the Taisha are Myojin Taisha.

Kami had ranks too: shinkai

The court gave ranks to kami just as it gave them to people. This is called shinkai, divine rank. Some kami rose all the way to Shoichii, First Rank, the same top rank that people could hold.

The red banners at Inari shrines that read “Shoichii Inari Daimyojin” are a remnant of this.

Layer 2: the 22 shrines the court relied on (Heian to Muromachi)

From the middle of the Heian period, the court gradually chose shrines to which it made special offerings, for rain-making prayers and in times of disaster. The number grew little by little until it reached 22, which is why they are called the Twenty-Two Shrines. The practice continued as a court ritual until the later Muromachi period.

Apart from Ise, all 22 lie around Kyoto, in Kyoto, Nara, Osaka, Shiga and Hyogo. There is not one in the Kanto region. The system was the capital relying on the shrines near the capital.

Layer 3: a ranking by province (from the end of the Heian period)

Ichinomiya

The highest-ranking shrine of each province is the Ichinomiya, and the next is the Ninomiya (see the basics article). Two points to add here.

  • There was never an official list made by the state. It was not a system fixed by law; it settled in as custom. That is why some provinces have two or more Ichinomiya.
  • Both the Ichinomiya and the Soja took shape in the later Heian period.

The Ichinomiya around Tokyo are read province by province in a separate article: Ichinomiya near Tokyo.

Soja

On taking up a post, a provincial governor (kokushi) visited the shrines of the whole province. To save that trouble, the kami of the province were enshrined together near the provincial capital (kokufu), and this is said to be the origin of the Soja. One of the earliest recorded examples is the Soja of Inaba, in 1099.

  • Okunitama Jinja in Fuchu, Tokyo, is the Soja of Musashi Province. It enshrines the Ichinomiya through Rokunomiya of Musashi together. The place name “Fuchu” also comes from the provincial capital (kokufu) that stood there.

Ichinomiya and Soja are separate systems. Ichinomiya is a ranking within a province; a Soja is a shrine that gathers the province's kami in one place.

Layer 4: the state's ranks (1871–1946)

In a proclamation of 1871, the Meiji government placed the shrines of the whole country into a system of state ranks.

RankWhat it means
Kanpeisha (large, medium, small)What it meansShrines that received offerings from the Imperial House at state rituals
Kokuheisha (large, medium, small)What it meansShrines that received offerings paid for from the national budget
Bekkaku Kanpeisha (special imperial shrines)What it meansA special class for shrines enshrining people who served the country. The first was Minatogawa Jinja in Kobe (Kusunoki Masashige)
Fusha, Kensha, Gosha, SonshaWhat it meansShrines of the prefecture, the district and the village
Mukakusha (unranked)What it meansShrines that fit none of the ranks. In 1945, 59,997 of the 109,712 shrines in the country, more than half.
  • The same words, a different meaning. In ancient times, Kanpei and Kokuhei described who made the offering; in the Meiji system they were ranks within a state system. The names are identical, so they are easily confused.
  • The system was abolished in 1946, after the war, when the state stopped administering shrines (see Japan and Shrines). That is why we now say “former Kanpei Taisha,” with “former” attached.

Layer 5: the labels still in use

The state ranks are gone, but two classifications are still in use.

Chokusaisha

Shrines to whose festivals an imperial envoy (chokushi) still comes. There are 16 today. Ise Jingu is treated separately from these 16.

  • Hikawa Jinja became a Chokusaisha in 1868. Emperor Meiji, who had moved to Tokyo, designated it the guardian shrine of Musashi Province and went to Omiya himself to hold a festival.
  • Of the 16, the ones on this map are Hikawa, Kashima, Katori, Meiji Jingu and Atsuta.

Beppyo shrines

Shrines that Jinja Honcho treats separately, in matters such as personnel. Jinja Honcho, the Association of Shinto Shrines, is a private religious corporation formed after the war, and most of the country's shrines belong to it. They are called Beppyo shrines because they are listed in the appendix (beppyo) to its regulations. The regulations were made in 1948, and at first only former Kanpeisha and Kokuheisha were listed. Shrines chosen for their history, buildings, number of priests, number of parishioners and so on were added later, and the total is now about 350.

This is not a state ranking but a classification inside one private organisation. It is easily confused with the old shrine ranks.

Easy mistakes

  • “Jingu” does not always mean the highest rank. It is a suffix that shows a link with the Imperial Family, and it is separate from age and from the modern ranks.
  • A shrine called “Taisha” was not necessarily a Kanpei Taisha. The suffix and the rank are different things (see the basics article).
  • There is no official list of Ichinomiya. It is a custom, not a system decided by the state.
  • The old shrine ranks no longer exist. That is why “former” is attached. The classifications still in use are the Chokusaisha and, inside Jinja Honcho, the Beppyo shrines.
  • Not being listed among the Shikinaisha does not mean a shrine is new.
